What a library card taught me about attention

My local library allows twelve books out at a time, for three weeks, with one renewal. As limits go, it's a generous one — I have never once hit the ceiling of twelve. But the three-week clock has shaped how I read more than almost any other habit I've built on purpose.

There's a specific kind of attention that only shows up under a deadline you didn't choose. A book I own outright can sit on a shelf for years, patiently, without ever making me feel anything about the delay. A borrowed book has an expiry date stamped in the front, and that date does something a to-do list never quite manages: it turns "I should read this" into "I have until the ninth."

The difference between owning and borrowing

Owning a book is a relationship with no schedule. Borrowing one is a relationship with a return date, and I've noticed I read borrowed books faster, more carefully, and more often to completion than books I own. Ownership feels safe in a way that quietly kills momentum — the book isn't going anywhere, so neither, often, am I.

This isn't really about libraries. It's about how open-ended commitments tend to lose to bounded ones, even when the bounded version is objectively more restrictive. A three-week library loan is a smaller offer than permanent ownership, and yet it's the one that actually gets read.

Where else this shows up

Once I noticed the pattern, I started seeing it everywhere. The project I "own" indefinitely — my own website, for instance — stays unfinished far longer than the project with an externally imposed deadline. The essay I'm allowed to write whenever I want is the one I never quite get to.

So this year I've been trying to borrow my own attention back, on purpose: giving myself library-style deadlines for things that technically don't need them. It feels a little artificial. It also, so far, works better than not doing it.

I don't think the lesson is "add fake deadlines to everything." It's closer to: notice which of your commitments feel borrowed, and consider whether the ones that don't might benefit from feeling that way too.
